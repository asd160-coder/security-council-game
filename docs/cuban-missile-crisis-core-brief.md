# Cuban Missile Crisis Security Council Game Core Brief

## Concept

This project is an educational strategy and role-play game inspired by Model United Nations and focused on the UN Security Council through the Cuban Missile Crisis.[cite:2] The game should develop critical thinking, consequential decision-making, negotiation, persuasion, historical understanding, and resolution writing while treating the subject matter with seriousness and sensitivity.[cite:2]

The first release is intentionally narrow: one crisis, one polished scenario, three playable historical roles, and a 20 to 25 minute classroom-friendly play session.[cite:2] The design should be visually compelling and interactive without becoming too text heavy, and it should scaffold students from guided choices toward a short culminating academic writing task.[cite:2]

## First release scope

The MVP focuses entirely on the Cuban Missile Crisis.[cite:2] The player selects one of three historical figures to role-play throughout the scenario: Robert Kennedy, Anatoly Dobrynin, or U Thant.[cite:2]

The scenario unfolds across multiple in-game days and includes:

- Authored dialogue choices in both formal and informal diplomatic scenes.[cite:2]
- Unlockable character dossiers and country or actor perspective cards.[cite:2]
- Incremental drafting moments that begin with selection and culminate in brief student writing.[cite:2]
- A final debrief that compares the player's path to the historical record.[cite:2]
- A map layer that highlights the global dimension of the crisis.[cite:2]
- Short archival radio or news snippets used sparingly to establish atmosphere.[cite:2]
- A sober consequence visualization to communicate the stakes of nuclear escalation.[cite:2]

## Core experience goals

The game should feel like a serious diplomatic simulation rather than a war game.[cite:2] Students should experience pressure, uncertainty, competing interests, and the challenge of persuasion, while also learning how language, timing, and strategic framing affect outcomes.[cite:2]

Three major experience goals should guide development:

- Seriousness: the crisis must feel weighty, never trivialized.[cite:2]
- Clarity: information should be layered through visuals, cards, maps, and short text, not dense exposition.[cite:2]
- Replayability: the same scenario should feel meaningfully different depending on which role the player chooses and how they negotiate.[cite:2]

## Playable roles

| Role | Perspective | Play emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Robert Kennedy | US executive pressure and back-channel diplomacy | Strategic credibility, private flexibility, managing escalation [cite:2] |
| Anatoly Dobrynin | Soviet signaling and strategic parity | Guarded bargaining, prestige, reciprocal concessions [cite:2] |
| U Thant | UN mediation and international legitimacy | De-escalation, procedural legitimacy, bridge-building [cite:2] |

## Learning progression

The scenario should scaffold students toward deeper academic thinking.[cite:2] Early interaction should be choice-based and highly supported, while later interaction should require more synthesis and a small amount of student-generated writing.[cite:2]

A strong progression looks like this:

1. Read concise updates and role information.[cite:2]
2. Make dialogue and strategy choices.[cite:2]
3. Unlock dossiers and actor or country cards that sharpen understanding.[cite:2]
4. Select and revise diplomatic language in small drafting tasks.[cite:2]
5. Complete a short final justification, amendment, or closing statement.[cite:2]
6. Reflect on the divergence between gameplay and history in the debrief.[cite:2]

## Core systems

The MVP should focus on a small number of interconnected systems.[cite:2] These systems should be elegant, legible, and meaningful rather than mechanically complex for their own sake.[cite:2]

Core systems:

- Dialogue choice system.[cite:2]
- Relationship and crisis trackers.[cite:2]
- Dossier unlock system.[cite:2]
- Country and actor perspective cards.[cite:2]
- Incremental drafting system.[cite:2]
- Branching consequence system.[cite:2]
- Map and event visualization layer.[cite:2]
- Final historical debrief.[cite:2]

Recommended trackers:

- Escalation.[cite:2]
- Legitimacy.[cite:2]
- Council trust.[cite:2]
- Diplomatic leverage.[cite:2]
- Civilian risk.[cite:2]

## Tone and presentation

The visual and narrative tone should be documentary-editorial rather than cartoonish or sensational.[cite:2] Archival images, dossier-style layouts, restrained typography, maps, subtle animation, and carefully chosen audio can create historical atmosphere without undermining seriousness.[cite:2]

Design guardrails:

- No celebratory war effects.
- No exaggerated destruction sequences for entertainment value.
- No overly gamified point systems.
- No silly fictionalization of the central historical actors.
- Fictional invention should mainly support aides, witnesses, and connective scenes, not rewrite the core nature of the crisis.[cite:2]

## Session structure

The game should be playable in 20 to 25 minutes.[cite:2] A likely structure is five in-game days plus debrief, with each day containing one major diplomatic scene, one secondary interaction, and one drafting moment.[cite:2]

Suggested arc:

- Day 0: role selection and opening briefing.[cite:2]
- Day 1: discovery and positioning.[cite:2]
- Day 2: public pressure and private channels.[cite:2]
- Day 3: escalation and leverage.[cite:2]
- Day 4: negotiation under pressure.[cite:2]
- Day 5: final decision and culminating writing task.[cite:2]
- Debrief: comparison to history and classroom reflection.[cite:2]

## Why this matters educationally

This concept is strong because it moves beyond passive history content and lets students inhabit diplomacy as a process of interpretation, persuasion, compromise, and consequence.[cite:2] It is especially well suited to classrooms that want game-based engagement without sacrificing seriousness, historical understanding, or writing-based follow-through.[cite:2]
