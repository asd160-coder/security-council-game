# Cuban Missile Crisis Security Council Game Design Packet

## Overview

This design packet outlines a first playable version of an educational strategy and role-play game based on Model United Nations, focused on the Security Council and centered on the Cuban Missile Crisis.[cite:2] The concept is designed to develop critical thinking, consequential decision-making, negotiation, persuasion, and historical understanding while treating the crisis with seriousness and gradually scaffolding students from accessible interaction toward greater academic rigor.[cite:2]

The first release is scoped as a 20 to 25 minute scenario that unfolds over multiple in-game days and allows the player to choose one of three playable historical figures: Robert Kennedy, Anatoly Dobrynin, or U Thant.[cite:2] The scenario combines authored dialogue choices, unlockable dossiers, country and actor perspective cards, incremental diplomatic drafting, and a final debrief comparing the player's path with historical events.[cite:2]

## Design pillars

### Serious but engaging

The game should feel tense, thoughtful, and strategic rather than playful in a frivolous sense.[cite:2] It should use the structure of a game to help students inhabit pressure, ambiguity, and competing priorities without turning nuclear crisis or human suffering into spectacle.[cite:2]

### Visual and readable

The interface should avoid long text blocks and instead rely on layered information design: portraits, maps, cards, short updates, timelines, relationship indicators, and concise dossier entries.[cite:2] This matches the user's goal of creating something graphically interesting and not too text heavy while preserving accuracy and intellectual weight.[cite:2]

### Scaffolding to rigor

The scenario should begin with guided interaction and short authored choices, then gradually move students toward more interpretive decisions and a short culminating writing task.[cite:2] This structure aligns with the user's broader educational preference for moving from accessibility and engagement toward more formal academic production.[cite:2]

### Replay through perspective

Replayability should come from role choice, diplomatic style, and branching outcomes rather than randomness or gamified chaos.[cite:2] Playing as Robert Kennedy, Dobrynin, or U Thant should meaningfully change what the player knows, values, and can plausibly attempt.[cite:2]

## Learning goals

By the end of a playthrough, students should be able to:

- Explain why the Cuban Missile Crisis created intense international pressure and risk.[cite:2]
- Distinguish among different diplomatic priorities, including deterrence, legitimacy, mediation, and face-saving compromise.[cite:2]
- Identify how the perspectives of major actors shape negotiation choices and public statements.[cite:2]
- Evaluate how language in statements or resolutions can escalate or de-escalate a crisis.[cite:2]
- Produce a short written diplomatic justification, amendment, or concluding position informed by the scenario.[cite:2]
- Compare a simulated outcome to the historical record through reflection and debrief.[cite:2]

## Audience and session design

The first playable version is best suited to secondary or early university learners in a teacher-guided environment, especially where one short session must contain both gameplay and discussion.[cite:2] Its current target is a 20 to 25 minute playthrough with multiple in-game days, followed by an end-of-game debrief and teacher reflection.[cite:2]

A typical classroom use model could be:

- 20 to 25 minutes of gameplay.[cite:2]
- 5 to 10 minutes of debrief and discussion.
- Optional extension work, such as rewriting a clause, comparing roles, or discussing what differed from history.

## Scenario frame

The first launch scenario focuses only on the Cuban Missile Crisis.[cite:2] This is a strong choice for a minimum viable product because it offers clear escalation, strong personalities, high-stakes diplomacy, back-channel tension, and multiple plausible pathways without requiring a large amount of fictional worldbuilding.[cite:2]

The scenario should be presented as a compressed multi-day diplomatic simulation. Each in-game day introduces new information, altered constraints, and changed diplomatic opportunities, allowing students to experience the crisis as an unfolding process rather than a single decision point.[cite:2]

## Playable roles

The player selects one of three historical figures at the start of the scenario.[cite:2] Each role should feel distinct in goals, tone, relationships, and available dialogue strategies.

| Role | Core perspective | Strengths in play | Key tension |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robert Kennedy | Internal US decision-making, credibility, strategic pressure | Access to executive urgency, back-channel possibilities, framing power | Balancing firmness with avoiding catastrophic escalation [cite:2] |
| Anatoly Dobrynin | Soviet diplomacy, strategic parity, guarded signaling | Ambiguity, bargaining, reading weakness, reciprocal concessions | Protecting Soviet interests without humiliation or uncontrolled escalation [cite:2] |
| U Thant | Mediation, UN legitimacy, de-escalation | Moral authority, neutral positioning, bridge-building | Seeking peace and procedural legitimacy amid great-power confrontation [cite:2] |

These three roles provide strong replay value because the same crisis feels different depending on whether the player is embedded in US executive pressure, Soviet diplomatic signaling, or UN-led mediation.[cite:2]

## Tone and ethical framing

The game should communicate that this is a simulation of real historical danger, not an entertaining war fantasy.[cite:2] The design should avoid celebratory war outcomes, flashy reward effects for escalation, or cartoonish representations of destruction.[cite:2]

Tone guidelines:

- Use restrained sound and motion.
- Frame consequences in terms such as escalation, legitimacy, trust, and civilian risk rather than score explosions.
- When showing nuclear-war consequence visuals, emphasize gravity and aftermath, not spectacle.
- Use real images carefully, with short captions or contextual notes.
- Keep fictional invention limited mainly to aides, witnesses, and connective scenes, as the user requested.[cite:2]

## Use of radio and archival media

Short radio or news clips from the period can be a highly effective atmospheric tool when used sparingly.[cite:2] They should function as transitional anchors that reinforce the time period and public mood, rather than as constant background noise.[cite:2]

Recommended uses:

- Opening role-selection briefing.
- Start of a new in-game day.
- After a major public-facing diplomatic statement.
- In the final debrief to contrast public messaging with private negotiation.

Implementation rules:

- Keep clips short.
- Make them skippable.
- Always provide captions and a source note.
- Follow each clip with a one-sentence explanation of why the clip matters.

## Map and consequence visualization

A map should appear at several points in the scenario to reinforce the global scale of the crisis and make abstract geopolitics more visible.[cite:2] This can be one of the most visually memorable features of the game if handled with restraint.[cite:2]

Suggested map uses:

- Opening global map showing the United States, Cuba, the Soviet Union, key maritime zones, and the United Nations.
- Day-to-day map updates showing crisis zones, naval movement, diplomatic routes, or intelligence changes.
- A missile trajectory or strike-range visualization to help students understand why the crisis felt immediate and destabilizing.[cite:2]
- A consequence animation that appears only at a high-escalation branch or in an educational debrief moment to show the potential human and global costs of nuclear war.[cite:2]

The consequence animation should be sober and schematic rather than sensational. A better approach is to use expanding zones, communication failure markers, fallout implications, and rapid changes in stability indicators instead of explosive cinematic imagery.[cite:2]

## Scene-by-scene blueprint

### Day 0: Role selection and opening briefing

The player selects Robert Kennedy, Anatoly Dobrynin, or U Thant.[cite:2] The screen introduces the crisis with a short timeline, one period radio clip, a world map, and three concise role cards that show perspective, immediate concern, and diplomatic style.[cite:2]

Player tasks:

- Select a role.
- Review the first perspective card.
- Choose an opening attitude, such as firm, cautious, conciliatory, or procedural.

Outputs:

- Starting relationship modifiers.
- First dossier unlocked.
- Initial drafting tray activated with one placeholder objective.

### Day 1: Discovery and positioning

The crisis briefing reveals the immediate seriousness of the missile issue and asks the player to make an early interpretive choice.[cite:2] This first day is about reading the situation, signaling intent, and deciding whether to prioritize pressure, caution, or mediation.[cite:2]

Scene components:

- Visual intelligence or briefing card.
- Formal or semi-formal response scene.
- First dossier unlock based on choice.
- Map view highlighting strategic geography.

Drafting task:

- Select the tone of an initial statement or clause from several authored options.[cite:2]

### Day 2: Public pressure and private channels

The player faces a split between official rhetoric and quieter diplomacy.[cite:2] One public-facing exchange is followed by a private conversation scene, where the player can test trust, probe motives, or preserve ambiguity.[cite:2]

Scene components:

- Public statement or chamber exchange.
- Private channel scene.
- One brief archival news clip to reflect public mood.
- New actor or country card unlocked.

Drafting task:

- Choose between possible wording frames for a statement or operative clause, with each affecting legitimacy and trust.[cite:2]

### Day 3: Escalation and leverage

Earlier choices begin to compound.[cite:2] Trust may have risen or collapsed, leverage may be stronger or weaker, and new pressure is introduced through an aide or witness perspective.[cite:2]

Scene components:

- Escalation update.
- Aide or witness intervention.
- A branching dialogue scene where the player chooses to intensify, pause, or test compromise.
- Map or consequence visualization showing the implications of worsening escalation.[cite:2]

Drafting task:

- Revise one earlier clause or select a compromise option that narrows the path toward either a harder or softer diplomatic posture.[cite:2]

### Day 4: Negotiation under pressure

This is the most strategically complex day.[cite:2] The player must coordinate dialogue, relationship management, and wording choices while facing reduced room for error.[cite:2]

Scene components:

- Multi-party negotiation scene.
- Unlockable dossier that reframes a major actor's motive or fear.
- Stronger visible impact of prior choices on who will listen or resist.

Drafting task:

- Assemble a short draft response from selected components rather than picking a single authored line.[cite:2]

### Day 5: Final decision and culminating task

The player makes the final diplomatic push and completes a short original writing task.[cite:2] This task might ask for a 2 to 4 sentence justification, amendment, or closing statement based on the route taken through the crisis.[cite:2]

Scene components:

- Final dialogue decision.
- Completion of draft language.
- Short student-written response.
- Branching ending.

Possible ending types:

- Uneasy de-escalation with preserved peace but damaged trust.[cite:2]
- Public diplomatic success with lingering private resentment.[cite:2]
- Temporary containment paired with legitimacy loss.[cite:2]
- Sharper confrontation caused by failed signaling or excessive pressure.[cite:2]

### Debrief

The debrief compares the player's choices to historical events and explains where the simulation diverged.[cite:2] It should highlight tradeoffs, diplomatic principles, and the relationship between rhetoric, timing, and outcome.[cite:2]

Debrief sections:

- What happened historically.
- What happened in this playthrough.
- Which choices shifted the path.
- What diplomatic skills were most important.
- Teacher discussion or reflection prompt.

## Gameplay loop

The gameplay loop should be easy to learn and consistent across the scenario.[cite:2] Repetition in structure frees student attention for strategic reasoning and historical interpretation.[cite:2]

Core loop:

1. Receive a crisis-day update.[cite:2]
2. Review cards, dossiers, and map changes.[cite:2]
3. Enter a formal or informal dialogue scene.[cite:2]
4. Make a diplomatic choice.[cite:2]
5. Observe relationship and consequence shifts.[cite:2]
6. Complete a drafting action.[cite:2]
7. Advance to the next in-game day.[cite:2]

This loop reinforces the design goal of equal weighting between authored dialogue and broader strategic consequence.[cite:2]

## Core mechanics

### Dialogue choices

Dialogue choices are the main interaction layer.[cite:2] They should be authored, short, strategically distinct, and influenced by role, previously unlocked knowledge, and prior relationships.[cite:2]

Choice types can include:

- Public condemnation.
- Private reassurance.
- Procedural delay.
- Moral appeal.
- Reciprocal concession.
- Strategic ambiguity.
- Face-saving compromise.[cite:2]

### Relationship system

A small set of visible diplomatic indicators should show how the player's choices are affecting the environment.[cite:2] These should not feel like arcade bars but like measured crisis variables.[cite:2]

Recommended trackers:

- Escalation.
- Legitimacy.
- Council trust.
- Diplomatic leverage.
- Civilian risk.[cite:2]

### Dossier unlock system

Dossiers unlock through interaction, not all at once.[cite:2] This makes background knowledge feel earned and gives students an incentive to pay close attention to people, motives, and turning points.[cite:2]

Unlock triggers can include:

- Choosing to consult an aide.
- Asking a probing question.
- Preserving a relationship long enough to gain trust.
- Noticing a contradiction in public and private positions.

### Drafting ladder

The drafting system should begin with low-friction selection and end with a small written culminating task.[cite:2] This preserves accessibility while still building toward authentic academic production.[cite:2]

Stages:

- Day 1: choose tone.
- Day 2: choose wording frame.
- Day 3: revise or amend.
- Day 4: assemble multiple parts.
- Day 5: write a short original justification or amendment.[cite:2]

### Branching consequence engine

Outcomes should not simply be good or bad.[cite:2] Instead, they should express different combinations of political, moral, and strategic cost.[cite:2]

Example outcome dimensions:

- Peace preserved but trust damaged.
- Legitimacy preserved but deterrence weakened.
- Tension lowered but alliances strained.
- Public success paired with hidden instability.[cite:2]

## Dossier system

Dossiers should be visually appealing, concise, and functionally useful.[cite:2] Each dossier should help the player interpret future choices more intelligently rather than just storing background information.[cite:2]

### Dossier template

Each dossier can include:

- Portrait or archival image.
- Name and role.
- Public stance.
- Likely priorities.
- Diplomatic style.
- Red lines.
- Historical context.
- Why this matters now.[cite:2]

### Suggested dossier list for MVP

- Robert Kennedy.[cite:2]
- Anatoly Dobrynin.[cite:2]
- U Thant.[cite:2]
- John F. Kennedy.
- Nikita Khrushchev.
- Adlai Stevenson.
- Fidel Castro.
- A military aide or intelligence analyst.
- A witness figure such as a journalist, observer, or diplomatic intermediary.
- A situation dossier on blockade, missiles, inspection, or world opinion.[cite:2]

### Sample dossier concepts

#### Robert Kennedy

- Public stance: serious, defensive, determined to prevent unacceptable strategic threat.
- Priorities: protect national credibility, avoid catastrophic war, preserve room for controlled negotiation.
- Style: direct but measured, capable of private channel diplomacy.
- Why it matters now: this role sits close to executive decision-making and can balance public firmness with private flexibility.

#### Anatoly Dobrynin

- Public stance: guarded, strategic, resistant to humiliation.
- Priorities: protect Soviet standing, preserve leverage, avoid one-sided retreat.
- Style: careful, ambiguous, responsive to reciprocal signals.
- Why it matters now: this role tests how deterrence and prestige shape diplomacy under pressure.

#### U Thant

- Public stance: de-escalatory, procedural, internationally minded.
- Priorities: reduce immediate danger, protect UN credibility, preserve a path to negotiation.
- Style: careful, morally serious, consensus-seeking.
- Why it matters now: this role teaches how legitimacy and mediation function when superpowers dominate the crisis.

## Country and actor cards

Country cards should be concise enough for quick consultation and strong enough to shape strategy.[cite:2] In this scenario, actor cards may be just as important as country cards because UN and individual diplomatic roles matter significantly.[cite:2]

### Card template

Each card can include:

- Country or actor.
- Core interests.
- Public message.
- Private concern.
- Preferred negotiation style.
- Red lines.
- Likely response to pressure.
- Possible concessions.[cite:2]

### MVP card list

- United States.[cite:2]
- Soviet Union.[cite:2]
- United Nations.[cite:2]
- Cuba.[cite:2]
- Optional observing or aligned power, depending on scope.

### Sample cards

#### United States

- Core interests: remove immediate strategic threat, maintain credibility, avoid appearing weak.
- Public message: unacceptable danger cannot be ignored.
- Private concern: escalation may become uncontrollable.
- Preferred style: firm signaling backed by selective flexibility.
- Red lines: perceived strategic humiliation or unchecked deployment.
- Possible concessions: private assurance, sequencing, face-saving framing.

#### Soviet Union

- Core interests: maintain strategic parity, avoid humiliation, protect influence.
- Public message: external pressure is unjust and destabilizing.
- Private concern: overreaction could trigger disastrous escalation.
- Preferred style: guarded bargaining, reciprocal movement, resistance to coercive framing.
- Red lines: unilateral retreat without strategic recognition.
- Possible concessions: mutual steps, private assurances, staged reduction.

#### United Nations

- Core interests: reduce immediate danger, preserve international legitimacy, create negotiating space.
- Public message: restraint and procedure are essential.
- Private concern: great-power politics may bypass institutional mediation.
- Preferred style: de-escalatory language, procedural pacing, legitimacy-building.
- Red lines: being reduced to symbolic irrelevance.
- Possible concessions: phased proposals, neutral framing, monitored pause.

#### Cuba

- Core interests: sovereignty, security, survival amid superpower confrontation.
- Public message: external threat justifies defensive posture.
- Private concern: being treated as a pawn by larger powers.
- Preferred style: assertive defense, suspicion of imposed compromise.
- Red lines: arrangements that ignore Cuban agency or security.
- Possible concessions: indirect guarantees, conditional de-escalation.

## Information architecture and interface layout

The interface should feel like an interactive diplomatic dossier rather than a conventional strategy game dashboard.[cite:2] Its purpose is clarity, atmosphere, and seriousness.[cite:2]

Suggested panel layout:

- Center: current scene and dialogue choices.
- Left: role card, map toggle, and unlocked dossiers.
- Right: relationship and crisis indicators.
- Bottom: drafting tray showing the current statement or resolution in progress.
- Overlay moments: radio clips, archival media, consequence visualization, and debrief comparisons.

## Visual style direction

The visual style should be documentary-editorial rather than cartoon or arcade.[cite:2] A restrained design approach using archival photography, clean typography, muted colors, and subtle motion will better support the subject matter.[cite:2]

Recommended art direction:

- Dossier-inspired panels.
- Cold War map textures.
- Timeline elements.
- Minimal but clear animation.
- Serious, understated sound design.

## Classroom integration

The game is strongest when followed by structured discussion.[cite:2] The built-in debrief should prepare students to move from gameplay impressions into historical reasoning and diplomatic analysis.[cite:2]

Possible extension activities:

- Compare the three playable roles.
- Rewrite a final clause from another perspective.
- Discuss whether de-escalation always requires compromise.
- Evaluate which dialogue choices preserved legitimacy best.
- Compare the game outcome to the historical record.

## MVP scope recommendation

To keep the project buildable while preserving quality, the first playable version should include:

- One crisis only: Cuban Missile Crisis.[cite:2]
- Three playable roles: Robert Kennedy, Anatoly Dobrynin, U Thant.[cite:2]
- Five in-game days plus debrief.[cite:2]
- Five major trackers.[cite:2]
- Eight to twelve dossiers.
- Four to six country or actor cards.
- One world map with several update states.[cite:2]
- One sober consequence visualization tied to high escalation or debrief.[cite:2]
- Short period media clips used sparingly.[cite:2]
- One final short writing task.[cite:2]

This scope is ambitious enough to feel original and substantial, but contained enough to prototype effectively as a first release.[cite:2]
